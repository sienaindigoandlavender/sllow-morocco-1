7 dresses, 6 stages, 1 week

A Moroccan wedding is not an event with a date. It is a sequence with a beginning weeks earlier and an end days later, and the part a guest attends is one night in the middle of it.

Updated 7 September 2026 Practice varies widely by region, family and budget. This is the common urban shape.

The sequence

The khotba is the formal asking, between families rather than individuals. The aqd is the legal act: the contract signed before two adouls, the traditional notaries, which is the moment the marriage actually exists. Everything else is celebration around that signature.

The henna night

The night before the feast belongs to the women. The bride’s hands and feet are worked in henna by a hennaya, in patterns that take hours, while the room sings. She does not do anything else that evening.

The chemistry is in the colour module: lawsone binds to keratin, so the stain deepens for two days after it is applied and then fades over two weeks. A bride is visibly married for a fortnight afterwards, which is part of the function.

The negafa is the central professional. She supplies the dresses, dresses the bride, controls the order of appearances and the timing of the entrances, and effectively directs the evening. Each dress is a different region: a Fassi kaftan, a Souss costume, an Amazigh one with the silver described elsewhere in this archive, a green Rifian set.

That progression is the point. A single bride wears the whole country in one night, and the guests read each costume as it appears.

Where the money goes

Catering and the room take half of any budget before anything else is decided, because the guest list is not really discretionary: a Moroccan wedding invites the extended family, the neighbours and the colleagues, and three hundred is a small one.

The rest is the performance. Negafa and wardrobe, the band, the film, and the amaria. Those are the elements a family scales up or down, and the ones a guest actually remembers.
