3 calendars, running at once

A Moroccan farmer sows by a Roman calendar, fasts by a lunar one and files his tax return by a third. All three are in daily use, none of them agrees with the others, and the oldest of them has been keeping time here since before the legions left.

Updated 7 September 2026 The Hijri ring is drawn at an illustrative position; it advances every year.

One year, three readings of it

The outer ring is the Gregorian year, which runs the state. The middle ring is the fellahi year, which runs the fields, offset by thirteen days because it is still keeping Julian time. The inner ring is the Hijri year, which runs the mosque and does not stay put: it is 354 days long, so it slips eleven days backwards against the other two, every year, for ever.

Rome left and the calendar stayed

The fellahi month names are Latin, worn down by fourteen centuries of Amazigh mouths. Yennayer is Ianuarius. Furar is Februarius. Ɣuct is Augustus. Ktuber is October.

Rome withdrew from the interior in 285 and the province was gone centuries before the Arab conquest. The towns took up the Hijri calendar for religion and administration; the countryside kept the solar one, because irrigation turns and sowing dates cannot follow a calendar that moves. The Julian year survived in North African fields after it had been abandoned across most of Europe.

Where Ramadan falls, over 33 years

Each dot is one year, placed by where the fast begins. The spiral runs outward from 2026, and it takes about thirty-three years to travel the whole way round and come back to the same season.

Which means a Moroccan born now will fast through every season of the year by the time they are middle-aged: through the short cold days of January and through fifteen hours of July heat, and back again. Anyone describing what Ramadan is like in Morocco is describing one turn of this spiral.

Two calendars in one household

Ahmed Skounti records that among the Ayt Moghad the women kept the lunar calendar and the men the solar one, so the tribe ran both simultaneously and each side of the household knew what day it was by a different reckoning. Which is what every Moroccan does now, with three.

There is also an Amazigh lunar calendar, later than the others, that gives Amazigh names to the Hijri months without changing their length. Its first month is not Muharram but Achour, after Ashura.

What is old and what is not

A distinction worth keeping straight. The agricultural calendar is genuinely ancient. The way its years are numbered is not.
