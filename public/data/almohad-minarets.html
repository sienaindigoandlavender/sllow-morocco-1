3 sisters, 2 countries, 1 design

The Koutoubia was the prototype. Within a decade the same proportions went up in Seville and in Rabat, on the orders of the same sultan. One is still a minaret, one was never finished, and one has been ringing bells for a cathedral since 1248.

Drawn to the same scale

The dashed outline over Rabat is what Hassan was meant to be. Yaqub al-Mansur intended the largest mosque in the western Islamic world and died in 1199, four years after the first stone. Work stopped that year and never resumed: 200 columns begun, a tower at roughly half its intended height, and nothing since.

A square shaft of pink Guéliz sandstone, restrained on the outside and lavish within, with interlacing polylobes, diamonds and chevrons worked into each face and no two faces alike. Almohad building generally is austere by intent, and the step-and-shoulder crenellation known as darj-wa-ktarf runs along the tops of their walls everywhere they built.

Ramps, not stairs

Hassan is climbed on an internal ramp rather than a staircase, which is the detail that tells you how it was built. Animals carried stone up the ramp to the working course. The same ramp meant the muezzin could ride a horse to the top to call the prayer.

One straight line

Marrakech, Rabat, Seville. Three cities of one empire, which is the thing the modern border hides: the Almohads ruled both shores and built the same building on each of them.

Who drew it

This is genuinely unsettled. Tradition names an architect called Jabir, sometimes identified with the astronomer and mathematician Jabir ibn Aflah, and credits him with all three towers. The attribution cannot be confirmed, and many scholars instead credit Ahmad Ben Basso, who is documented as the designer of the Koutoubia.
