The oven is lit at 3.30

A medina with no cars in it has to move everything by hand, which means it runs on a schedule most visitors never see. The working day starts before the first call to prayer, and by the time the souks open the hardest part of it is already done.

Updated 7 September 2026 Marrakech timings, in summer. Winter runs about an hour later at both ends.

Twenty-four hours, drawn round

Eight bands, one ring each, on a dial that shades from night to day. Notice how much of the ring is occupied before eight in the morning, and that the only genuinely empty hour is the one before the oven is lit.

The hours

03:30
The farran lights the oven
The neighbourhood bread oven is fired first, because everything downstream waits on it. It takes an hour to come up to heat.
04:15
The first carts
No vehicle over a certain size gets in, so everything arrives on a handcart or a donkey. This is the window when the lanes are empty enough to move volume.
05:00
Fajr
The muezzins, not quite in unison. In the medina you hear four or five at different distances, a few seconds apart.
05:30
The mules
Rubbish out, building material in. The lanes are too narrow for anything else, and this is when they are clear.
06:30
Dough arrives
Households and small kitchens carry unbaked loaves to the oven on wooden boards, each marked so it comes back to the right family.
08:00
Shutters
The first shops. Cafés before workshops, workshops before souks.
09:00
The souks open properly
And the sequence in the trade quarters starts: the noisy trades early, the fine ones later.
13:00
The long middle
Shutters come down. In summer the lanes empty for three hours and the medina belongs to cats.
16:00
Second opening
Which is the real trading afternoon, and runs later than most visitors expect.
18:30
The square fills
Food stalls assemble in about forty minutes, from nothing to a functioning night market.
23:00
The last cart
Stalls break down, and the carts that brought everything in take it back out.
02:00
The quiet hour
The only hour with almost nothing in it. Then the oven is lit again.

Why it has to work this way

No cars
The lanes were laid out for a loaded animal, and still are
Handcart
Everything in and everything out, on two wheels or four legs
Before dawn
The only window when the lanes are clear enough to move volume
One oven
Per quarter, shared, and the whole morning is sequenced around it

A city that cannot be driven through has to be supplied in the gaps. That is the entire logic of the schedule: bulk moves while the lanes are empty, trade happens when they are full, and the two never overlap.

The oven, which is the clock

The farran fires his oven around half past three because it takes an hour to reach temperature and the first loaves have to be back with families before the working day. Households do not bake at home. They make the dough, carry it on a board, and collect it later.

Each board is marked, and the farran knows every mark. That system is described at length elsewhere in this archive; what the dial adds is where it sits in the day. Everything else in the medina is arranged around a fire that is lit while it is still dark.

What a visitor sees, and when

The medina most people describe is the one between ten in the morning and seven in the evening, which is the part designed to be seen. The other sixteen hours are the part that makes it possible.

Anyone awake at five will find the lanes belong to mules, bread boards and the men who have been up for two hours. It is the only time the geometry of the place is legible, because there is nobody in the way.
