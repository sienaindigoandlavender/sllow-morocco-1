11 of the 24 hours survive

Ziryab is said to have built 24 suites, one for each hour of the day, each in its own mode. Eleven came through intact. The music was never written down, so what reached the present did so inside people, and an eighteenth-century poet in Fes went round collecting what the old musicians could still remember.

Each nuba carries the name of the mode it is played in: Ramal al-Maya, al-Isbihan, al-Maya, Rasd al-Dhil, al-Istihlal, al-Hijaz al-Kabir, and the rest. A mode here is not only a scale but the set of rules by which a piece in it may be composed, which is why losing one loses more than a melody.

The shape of one

Five rhythmic movements, called mizan, each with its own cycle, running slow to fast across six or seven hours. Every mizan opens with an instrumental prelude — a tuashia, m’shaliya or bughya — and can then carry as many as twenty sung pieces, the sana’a. Nobody performs the whole arc often. It is a form built for a night, not a concert.

The man who saved it

The tradition was oral. It travelled with the exiles who left Iberia, and by the eighteenth century a good deal of it was held only in the memories of ageing musicians. Muhammad al-Haik, a poet, went and wrote down whatever they could still produce.

The Moroccan repertoire is still called al-Haik after him. What survives is what a group of old men in Fes could remember on the days he came to ask, which is a thin thread for a thousand-year-old art to have hung from and the reason there are gaps on the clock above.

Musicologists generally hold the Moroccan version closest to the original, on the grounds that it was the most isolated after 1492 and had the least opportunity to drift.

An honest disagreement about the count

The commonest figure is eleven complete nubat and five incomplete. Some authorities give four complete and seven fragmentary instead. The difference turns on how much of a suite has to be intact before it counts as whole, and there is no agreed threshold.

Either way, more than half of what Ziryab is said to have built is gone, and it went because nobody wrote it down.
