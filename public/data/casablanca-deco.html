Néo-mauresque came from Algeria, and the planners hated it

The style visitors read as the authentic look of colonial Casablanca was invented in Algiers and Tunis decades earlier, arrived here second-hand, and was fought by the two men who planned the city. Lyautey and Prost thought it was decoration stuck onto a European building.

The objection, drawn

The complaint was specific. Regular floors, symmetrical bays and classical circulation — a European building — with round or pointed arches, azulejos-style faience panels, mashrabiya cast in cement and green roof tiles applied to the outside of it. Ornament without plan.

It is worth noticing whose argument that was. The debate ran entirely between French planners and French architects about which European idea of Morocco should be built.

The doctrine underneath

Lyautey’s instruction was to build the new city beside the medina rather than through it. The ville nouvelle went up on open ground, the old city was left standing, and the two were joined rather than merged.

Whatever else is true of the protectorate, that decision is why the medinas of Fes, Marrakech and Rabat survive as continuous urban fabric. Cities elsewhere in the Maghreb that were driven through did not keep theirs.

The Habous is a medina that was designed

The strangest thing in the city. Laprade and his colleagues were asked to build a new indigenous quarter from nothing, in traditional Moroccan idiom, using European planning method: a purpose-built medina with surveyed streets and a drainage scheme.

It works, which is the uncomfortable part. People live in it, the market functions, and it reads as older than it is. Whether that makes it a successful piece of urbanism or an accomplished forgery is a question the quarter poses and does not answer.

UNESCO has described Casablanca as an experimental architectural laboratory of the twentieth century, which is accurate and slightly cold. Successive European movements were tried out here at full scale, one after another, on a city that had no say in the brief.

Look up. Almost every ground floor on Boulevard Mohammed V has been altered into shopfronts. The building only survives above the first floor.
