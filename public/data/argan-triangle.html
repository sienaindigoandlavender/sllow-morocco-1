A tree that grows nowhere else

Argania spinosa covers 800,000 hectares between Essaouira and the Souss and exists at scale nowhere else on earth. The women who work it built a cooperative model the FAO holds up as a template. The open question is how much of what the oil earns stays in the country.

The kernel is the bottleneck. Each fruit holds a stone, each stone holds one to three kernels, and the stone is cracked between two rocks by hand because no machine has been made that does it without breaking what is inside.

Most of the value is added after the oil leaves Morocco, in bottling and branding. Moving more of that work to the Souss, where the knowledge already is, would move the margin with it.

What the lockdown did

With the cooperatives closed in 2020, the fruit under the trees was collected and stored by others. When the doors opened again there was nothing left on the ground, and the price of a kilo of fruit had gone from two dirhams to twelve.

Fifty-four women are members there. They now take turns, four called in on a day when an order comes.

The tree grows in one place

Argania spinosa covers roughly 800,000 hectares of semi-arid land between Essaouira and the Souss, and nowhere else at any scale. It lives two hundred years, survives droughts that kill everything around it, and holds the line against the desert moving north.

UNESCO lists the practices and knowledge around the argan tree as Intangible Cultural Heritage, and the FAO treats the forest as a model for sustainable forest management.

Two oils, one tree

GradeHow it differs
CulinaryKernels roasted before pressing. Deep gold, strongly nutty. Poured over couscous, or ground with almond and honey into amlou
CosmeticUnroasted, cold pressed. Paler, almost odourless. More kernels needed for the same volume, which is why it costs more

A stall selling both from the same bottle is selling one of them. The culinary version smells of roasted nuts from a metre away; the cosmetic version smells of very little.
