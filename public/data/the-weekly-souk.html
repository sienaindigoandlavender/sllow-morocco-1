A map of Morocco is a timetable

Hundreds of Moroccan towns carry the name of the day their market is held. Read the road sign and you know when to come. The place is not a place that happens to have a souk; it is a souk that grew a town around itself.

Updated 7 September 2026 Toponymy is stable. Individual market days occasionally shift by local decision.

The week, and the towns on it

Arabic counts its days. Had is one, tnine two, tleta three, arba four, khemis five. Friday breaks the sequence and becomes jemaa, the gathering, because that is the congregational prayer. Saturday is sebt, the sabbath, which is the same word as the Hebrew one and arrived by the same route.

How the name is built

The full form is souk, then the day, then the people. Souk Tnine des Beni Harden is the Monday market of the Beni Harden. Souk Khemis des Beni Arouss is the Thursday market of the Beni Arouss. The name records who trades there as well as when, which is why so many survive in full.

The day itself

Traders leave before dawn and walk or drive in, set up at first light, and begin dismantling in the early afternoon so that everyone can be home before dark. A rural souk is over by the time a city market is busy.

What a souk actually is

Buying and selling is the smallest part of it. The weekly market is the one day the surrounding valleys are in the same place, which makes it the high street, the clinic, the garage and the notice board at once.

The other kind of souk

City souks are named for the trade rather than the day, because they open every morning and do not need one. Souk el Attarine is the spice market, and there is one in Marrakech, Tunis, Damascus, Amman, Beirut and Jerusalem, all carrying the same name for the same reason.

Which is the distinction underneath the whole thing. A city souk is arranged in space, by trade, as set out elsewhere in this archive. A rural souk is arranged in time, by day, across a whole province, so that no two neighbouring towns compete for the same crowd.
