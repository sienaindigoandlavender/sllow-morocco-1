10 years to cut a tile

A maallem is not a job title you apply for. It is the end of an apprenticeship that begins in childhood and runs for years before anyone is trusted to work unsupervised, and the length is different for every trade.

How long each one takes

The zellige cutter is longest because the work is subtractive and unforgiving: a glazed blank is chipped to shape face-down, by eye, with a hammer, and a piece cut wrong is finished. Ten years is how long it takes to stop making that mistake.

The words

Maallem
Master. The one who takes responsibility for the work and the apprentices
Sanaa
The craft itself. The same root as the word for making
Amin
The elected head of a trade guild, historically responsible for standards
Fondouk
The courtyard building where a trade stored goods, stabled animals and worked

The guild structure is older than any of the workshops in it. Each trade had an amin who set quality, arbitrated disputes and answered to the authorities for the whole craft, which is why the trades cluster: they were organised as bodies, not as businesses.

Why the trades sit where they sit

A medina is zoned, and the logic is smell and fire. Booksellers, perfumers and candle makers take the ground nearest the great mosque, because they are quiet and clean and their customers are already there. Cloth, slippers, jewellery and spices form the middle.

The dyers, tanners, smiths and potters are pushed out to the gates and the water, because of what they smell like and what they burn. The Koutoubia takes its name from the booksellers who worked under it; the tanneries sit on the river. Nothing about that arrangement is accidental.

The four that build a room

TradeWhat it does
ZelligeThe floor and the lower wall. Glazed terracotta cut into shaped pieces and laid face-down into a pattern the setter holds in his head
GebsThe band above it. Plaster applied wet and carved before it sets, so the whole panel is one hardening race
ZouakThe ceiling. Cedar, assembled and then painted in fine geometric and floral work
NejjarinThe joinery holding all of it: doors, screens, and the frames the others fill

A traditional Moroccan interior is four trades stacked vertically, in that order, from the floor to the ceiling. Each takes over where the last one stops, and none of them can begin until the one below is finished.

What the hand still does better

Zellige is cut by hand because the pieces are not identical. Slight irregularity in each tile is what makes a laid panel read as a single surface rather than a grid, and a machine-cut version looks wrong in a way most people notice without being able to name.

The same is true of a hand-turned spindle, a coil-built pot and a knotted carpet. The variation is not tolerated. It is the reason the object looks the way it does.
