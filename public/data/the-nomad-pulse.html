A house of goat hair, and a parliament with no building

The Ait Atta are one of the great confederations of the south, and for centuries they governed themselves without a capital, a palace or a permanent ruler. The decisions were taken in assembly, the chief was rotated, and the whole apparatus of state fitted inside a tent.

Updated 7 September 2026 Descriptive of traditional practice. Most families are now settled or partly settled.

The tent, and the assembly

The cloth is woven in long strips of goat hair and camel wool and sewn edge to edge, so a tent grows as a household grows. Goat hair is the material because it swells when wet and closes the weave against rain, then dries and opens again to let air through in heat. It is a fabric that changes its own porosity.

Inside, a divider separates the household side from the side where guests are received. That partition is the plan of the whole building, and it is the same idea as the courtyard house: a private half and a public half, with one threshold between them.

How they governed

Jmaa
The assembly. Heads of household, deciding together
Rotation
The chieftaincy passed between the five branches rather than staying in one
One year
The traditional term, after which it moved on
Elected out
The branch due to hold it stood aside while the others chose from among it

The last of those is the ingenious part. When it was a branch’s turn to supply the chief, the members of that branch did not vote; the other branches chose which of them it would be. A leader was therefore picked by the people he would have least reason to favour.

Alongside it ran the orf, customary law, and the agdal rules on pasture described in the transhumance module. None of it required a building, a permanent official or a written constitution.

The oases and the market townsWhere the wool, meat and butter went, and where the grain and tea came from

They were never isolated. A pastoral household needs grain it does not grow and metal it cannot make, so the movement between mountain and market is part of the system rather than an interruption to it. The souk day described elsewhere in this archive is when that exchange happened.

What a household carries

ObjectWhy
The tent itselfWoven by the women of the household, in strips, and repaired the same way
The loomGround-staked, dismantled in minutes, and the source of the tent, the blankets and the carpets
Grinding stonesHeavy, and taken anyway. Flour is daily
Copper and enamel vesselsBought at a souk, kept for decades, and the visible wealth of the household
SilverWorn rather than stored. The fibulae described elsewhere in this archive are portable capital

Nothing in that list is decorative and nothing is disposable. A household that moves twice a year owns what it can justify carrying, and everything it owns has to survive the journey and be worth making again.

What it is now

Most Ait Atta families are settled, or move only seasonally with part of the household, and the children are in school in the valley. The tents on the high pasture in summer are fewer than they were, and they are still there.

The confederation itself has not gone anywhere. The lineages, the customary pasture rules and the assemblies continue in the places where they still make sense, alongside the ordinary institutions of a modern state, which is how most old arrangements survive anywhere.
