5 people, 300,000 years old, 90 km from Marrakech

Barite miners blew open a limestone cave at Jbel Irhoud in 1960. What came out of it is now the oldest securely dated evidence of our own species anywhere on earth, and it is a hundred thousand years older than anything found in East Africa.

Updated 7 September 2026 Age is published as 300,000 to 350,000 years. The classification is not universally accepted; see below.

The same bones, dated three times

Nothing about the fossils changed. In 1961 they were read as Neanderthals of about forty thousand years, and filed as a curiosity because they fitted no theory. Improved dating in the 1990s pushed them back. In 2017 a team under Jean-Jacques Hublin of the Max Planck Institute and Abdelouahed Ben-Ncer of INSAP in Rabat published two papers in Nature putting them at three hundred thousand.

A modern face on an archaic head

This is the finding that matters more than the date. The face and jaw are already within the range of people alive now. The braincase is long and low and looks archaic. Which means the face arrived first and the shape of the brain went on changing afterwards, inside our own lineage, for another quarter of a million years.

The first almost-complete adult mandible from the site came out of this work. The animals tell you the landscape: this was grassland with grazing herds, not the dry country around Jbel Irhoud today.

What it did to the map

Before 2017 the oldest securely dated Homo sapiens came from Omo Kibish in Ethiopia at around 195,000 years, and the picture was an East African cradle. Jbel Irhoud is five thousand kilometres away and a hundred thousand years older, and it makes better sense of Florisbad in South Africa at around 260,000.

Hublin’s summary is the line everyone quotes: there is no Garden of Eden in Africa, or if there is, it is Africa. Our species appears to have emerged across the continent rather than at a point, and to have dispersed within Africa long before it left.

What is disputed

Not everyone accepts the classification. John Hawks has pointed out that the Jbel Irhoud material was not compared with Homo antecessor, known from 800,000-year-old fossils in Spain. On that reading, the face might be inherited from an antecessor-like population rather than evolving toward us, which would make Irhoud a cousin rather than an ancestor.

The dating is not in serious question. The label is. Both positions are on the record, and the material sits at a point where the definition of our own species stops being obvious.
