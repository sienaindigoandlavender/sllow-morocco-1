60% of Britain’s tinned sardines

Morocco is the largest exporter of canned sardines on earth and sells to 130 countries. The reason sits three kilometres off the coast: a current and a wind that between them pull cold, fertile water up from the deep along 3,500 kilometres of Atlantic shore.

Updated 7 September 2026 Latest: 2024 landings and the Casablanca port inauguration, September 2025.

Why the fish are there

The Canary Current runs south along the coast and the prevailing wind runs with it. Between them they push surface water away from the land, and water has to come from somewhere to replace it, so it rises from below carrying the nutrients that have been sinking for years. Plankton bloom in the sunlight, sardines eat the plankton, and everything else eats the sardines.

It is one of four great upwelling systems in the world’s oceans, and it is strongest off the southern coast.

Total capture that year came to 1,556,406 tonnes, out of an estimated sustainable potential of about 1.6 million. The margin is thin, which is why closures are imposed on the sardine fishery in some seasons.

Fisheries are 58% of Morocco’s agro-food exports and about 7% of all exports. Morocco is also the world’s leading exporter of semi-preserved anchovies and among the leading exporters of octopus.
