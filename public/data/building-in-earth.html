Pisé below, adobe above

The heavy rammed earth carries the ground floor and lighter sun-dried brick goes on top, so the load on the lower wall stays inside what the material will bear. That single decision is why these towers stand three and four storeys high, and it was arrived at without a calculation.

The wall, in section

Everything above the ground line exists to keep water off what is below it. The render is sacrificial and gets renewed; the parapet stops rain sitting on the wall head; the spout throws it clear of the face. An earthen building fails from the top down and from the outside in, and almost never from the middle.

3 words people mix up

Ksar
A whole fortified village inside one defensive wall with corner towers. Houses, granaries, a mosque, public squares. Plural ksour.
Kasbah
In the south, a single fortified family tower-house. A ksar contains several. Elsewhere the word can mean a citadel quarter, which is where the confusion starts.
Riad
An urban courtyard house with a garden at its centre, in a medina. Unrelated to either of the above, and not a defensive building at all.

What goes into the mix

Clay
Damp earth from within walking distance of the site
Straw
Binds the mix and controls shrinkage as it dries
Water
Enough to compact, not enough to slump
Lime
Sometimes, with gravel, to stabilise the mix

Pisé is known as tabia and as al-luh. The mix goes into removable timber formwork and is rammed with a wooden pestle until it is dense, then the box moves up and the next lift goes on once the one below has dried. The horizontal lines visible across an old wall are those lifts.

Thick earth walls also do the thermal work. In country that swings between a scorching afternoon and a cold night, mass is the insulation.

A building that is never finished

Earthen architecture survives on maintenance rather than on durability. Left alone it returns to the ground over a couple of centuries, and stands indefinitely if the render is renewed each year. Occupation and survival are the same thing.

The technique did not stay in the past

Rammed earth is back in contemporary architecture as a low-carbon structural material, in the work of Martin Rauch in Austria and Francis Kéré in Burkina Faso and elsewhere. The material is dug on site, needs no firing, and insulates by mass.

Which makes the ksour of the Draa and the Ounila valleys a working reference rather than a ruin: several centuries of accumulated knowledge about the load-bearing limits of earth, recorded in the buildings themselves.
