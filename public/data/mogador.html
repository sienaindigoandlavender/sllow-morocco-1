10 families, 40 synagogues, 1 planned port

Essaouira was drawn on paper before it existed. A sultan wanted a port that could trade with Europe, so he commissioned a French military architect, built it with French prisoners, and then invited ten Jewish merchant families to come and run it.

Updated 7 September 2026 Foundation is dated 1764 and 1765 in different accounts; both are given.

The plan

Cornut’s own map is dated 25 October 1767 and uses two colours: what had been built by the time he left, and what was still only projected. The medina grid and the kasbah were up; the port front and the mellah were still drawings. The streets in the built portion are still walkable and still recognisable from the sheet.

Four quadrants, a grid, and bastioned walls modelled on Saint-Malo in the manner of Vauban. It is a European fortified town plan, executed in Morocco, for a Moroccan sultan, in order to make foreign merchants comfortable enough to move in.

Accounts differ on whether Cornut was hired or held. Britannica calls him a French captive; other records have him invited from Gibraltar and leaving of his own accord. What is not disputed is who did the labour.

The merchants

From about 1764 the sultan recruited roughly ten leading Jewish merchant families out of Tangier, Agadir, Marrakech and the north, and made them the tujjar as-sultan: the sultan’s traders. Corcos, Afriat, Knafo, Pinto. Most were descended from families expelled from Andalusia in the 1490s, and they were chosen because they already had correspondents in Europe.

That last date belongs in the same story. Recognition of the new American republic was a trade decision, taken by the same sultan, in service of the same port. Essaouira was built to be the place where Morocco met the Atlantic world, and recognising a new Atlantic country was part of the plan.

The name is older than any of it. Mogador is a worn-down Amazigh word meaning safe anchorage, which is the only thing about the site that was never designed.

What is left of the community

The Jewish population left in the middle of the twentieth century, in the movement described elsewhere in this archive. What remains is the Mellah and the older Mellah Kdim beside the kasbah, the Haim Pinto and Slat Lkahal synagogues, the cemeteries above the sea, and Bayt Dakira, the House of Memory, opened in 2020.

The Pinto hiloula still runs each September and still brings families back. It is the one part of the traffic the sultan set up that has never entirely stopped.
