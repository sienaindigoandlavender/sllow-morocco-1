1,000 years without a change of method

In the fourteenth century the historian Ali Ibn Abi Zar counted eighty-six working tanneries inside the Fes medina. Chouara is one of three still running, on the same site and the same chemistry it used in the eleventh century, and it still supplies the leather trade of a working city.

Updated 7 September 2026 Historical, with current practice observed on site.

One hide, thirty days

The white vats come first: quicklime, salt, water, cow urine and pigeon droppings. The ammonia loosens the hair and softens the skin so it will take dye later. Men stand thigh-deep and tread the hides with their feet. That mixture is the smell, and there is no version of the process without it.

The colours in the pits

Every vat you can see from the terrace is a plant, a mineral or a spice. The palette changes through the year with what is available and with whatever the workshops have ordered.

Fig paste and oil go into the dye vats with the hides, to soften the leather and make it hold up to wear.

The three that remain are the three best sited. Chouara is beside the river that supplied its water and at the edge of the quarter, exactly where a medina puts a trade that needs volume and space. Eight centuries of consolidation left the ones that worked.
