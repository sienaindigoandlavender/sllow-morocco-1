A pound of salt for an ounce of gold

Salt does not occur in tropical West Africa and gold does. That single asymmetry ran a trade route across the largest desert on earth for more than two thousand years, and Morocco sat at the northern end of it.

Camels per caravan

The scale grew with the trade. By the eleventh century a thousand camels was typical; by the thirteenth, five to ten thousand was common; and caravans leaving Sijilmasa are recorded at up to twenty thousand.

Why a camel and not a horse

North Africa's own camel went extinct in the Stone Age. The dromedary arrived around 300 BCE, and the Amazigh improved the saddle, which is the innovation that turned a barrier into a road.

One route, south from Morocco

A caravan leaving Sijilmasa carried salt from Taghaza with foodstuffs, cloth and perfume from the Maghreb, and returned with gold, ivory, hides and enslaved people. The southern terminus shifted over time: Awdaghost under the Almoravids, Walata under the Almohads.

Where the gold ended up

By the twelfth century Sudanese gold refined at Sijilmasa was reaching Europe and being struck into European coinage. The metal in Moroccan and European coins of the period is of identical quality, which is the evidence for how much of it moved.

Five rendezvous points assembled the caravans themselves: Sijilmasa, In Salah, Wargla, Ghadames and Aujila. Merchants gathered money, camels, drivers, guides, water and provisions at each before setting out.

What the gold paid for

The route did not end at the Mediterranean. West African gold financed European coinage for three centuries, and the flow of it shaped monetary policy in places that had never heard of Timbuktu.
