6 names for 1 man

He was born in Granada, raised in Fes, sent to Timbuktu by a sultan, taken at sea by Christian corsairs, and made to write the definitive European account of Africa in Italian, in Rome, for the Pope who baptised him. Each stage of that came with a new name.

Updated 7 September 2026 Dates of birth, capture and death are all given as ranges in the sources.

The names, in order

The birth name carries two cities: al-Fasi for Fes, al-Gharnati for Granada. He kept the second one in the Arabic name he chose for himself in Rome, Yuhanna al-Asad al-Gharnati, thirty years after his family had been expelled from the place. The name Europe settled on erases both cities and replaces them with a continent.

Where he went, and where he was taken

Granada to Fes as a child, in the expulsion described elsewhere in this archive. Then Timbuktu and Gao with his uncle around 1510, on an embassy to the Songhai court. Then Cairo and up the Nile to Aswan in 1516 and 1517. He was sailing home in 1518 when he was intercepted — near Djerba in some accounts, more probably near Crete — and imprisoned on Rhodes.

What normally happened to a captive like him

The usual fate of an unransomed Muslim captive was the oar bench of a Christian galley. He was spared it because his captors worked out what they had: a man who had crossed the Sahara and the Nile on state business, spoke several languages, and had been trained in law at al-Qarawiyyin.

So he was moved to the Castel Sant’Angelo and presented to Pope Leo X in January 1520. He was freed within about a year, baptised with the Pope standing sponsor, and given a pension to make him stay. He taught Arabic, learned Latin and Italian, and is said to have taught at Bologna.

He probably worked from an earlier Arabic draft. What survives is a book about the Muslim world written for Christian readers by a man who belonged to the first and was living, on a pension, among the second. Europe read Africa through him until the nineteenth century.

Both books are the same kind of object and were made under opposite conditions. It is worth reading the second knowing who commissioned it.

How much of it is true

The North African material stands up well. His Fes and his Marrakech match the archaeology and the contemporary Arabic itineraries, down to caravan routes and the standing fabric of al-Qarawiyyin, which suggests direct familiarity rather than compilation.

The sub-Saharan material attracts more scrutiny. His account of a stay at Timbuktu around 1510 to 1513, and of markets heavy with gold and manuscripts, runs ahead of what Portuguese coastal expeditions of 1515 to 1520 recorded. Whether that is exaggeration, second-hand reporting, or an accurate picture of a city those expeditions never reached is still argued.
