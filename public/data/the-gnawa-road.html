The music came up the same road as the gold

Gnawa is not indigenous to Morocco. It arrived with people taken north across the Sahara from Mali, Senegal and the Ghana empires, along the caravan routes that carried salt one way and gold the other.

Where it came from

The dashed line is the route. Bambara, Hausa and Songhai rhythms travelled it from the tenth century to the nineteenth, and met North African Sufism at the other end.

One word, three languages, and a country

The name traces back through Amazigh rather than Arabic, and the same root produced a name that European mapmakers put on the West African coast in the fourteenth century and never took off again.

Three instruments

Guembri
Also sintir, hajhouj. Played by the maalem.
Three-stringed bass lute, body covered in camel skin. Carries the melody and the low drone underneath everything.
Qraqeb
Krakeb in Darija. Played in pairs.
Heavy iron castanets. The sound is usually described as galloping horses, and sometimes as chains.
Tbel
Struck with sticks.
Large double-headed drum. Used in procession more than in the ceremony itself.

The lila and the festival

Two different things carry the same music. The lila is an all-night healing ceremony that runs sunset to dawn, organised around colours and the spirits attached to them. The festival is three days in June with half a million people in a town of eighty thousand.

The other diaspora

The same displacement that produced Gnawa in Morocco produced Vodou in Haiti, Candomblé in Brazil, Santería in Cuba and the blues in the Mississippi Delta. Different oceans, different centuries, and a recognisably similar answer: possession, call and response, and a musician who works as a healer.
