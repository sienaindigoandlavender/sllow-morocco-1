At the other end of the empire, no wall

Rome ran a frontier across northern Britain and another across northern Morocco. In Britain it built a wall. Here it built a filter: a scatter of forts, ditches and watchtowers with gaps in it, because the point was to regulate movement rather than stop it.

Updated 7 September 2026 Historical. Excavation at several sites is incomplete.

2 frontiers, 2 answers

Northern Britain
Hadrian’s Wall
A continuous barrier, 117 kilometres, coast to coast. Stone, ditch, milecastles, turrets. A line drawn to be held.
Northern Morocco
A network, not a line
Forts, a fossatum, a rampart and watchtowers at Sala, but no continuous defensive wall anywhere. Volubilis was ringed by camps at Tocolosida and Ain Chkour and left otherwise open.

Rome traded with the tribes beyond the line and needed them to be able to cross it. A filter passes things through on terms, which is what a trading frontier needs.

How far it actually reached

Not far. The province was a coastal strip and one inland city, and the Atlas was never crossed. Volubilis is as deep as the empire ever got in the far west.

Mogador, on the Atlantic well south of the province, was a purple-dye island rather than a governed town. The Phoenicians had been on this coast since the eighth century BCE, six hundred years before Rome arrived.

The dates

8th c. BCE
Phoenicians settle the coast
Sailing from Tyre, by way of Carthage. Lixus and Mogador are theirs long before they are anyone else’s.
33–25 BCE
Augustus founds three colonies
Banasa among them, settled with veterans of Actium, built on top of an Amazigh village that had been there since the fourth century BCE.
40–42 CE
Mauretania Tingitana becomes a province
Capital at Tingis, which is Tangier. Named for it.
217
The triumphal arch at Volubilis
Raised for Caracalla and his mother Julia Domna. It is still standing.
285
Diocletian pulls the line back
The province is reduced to the territory north of the Lixus river and Banasa is abandoned, though Volubilis carries on.
285–788
Five centuries of afterwards
Amazigh and Latin-speaking Christian communities go on living at Volubilis for half a millennium after Rome leaves.
788
Idris I arrives
He uses Volubilis as his base before founding Fes. Moulay Idriss, on the hill above, replaces it as the regional centre.
17th c.
Moulay Ismail takes the stone
Dressed stone from Volubilis goes into the palace complex at Meknes. What survives is what nobody found worth carrying away.
1755
The Lisbon earthquake
It reaches Morocco and brings down a great deal of what was left standing.
1887
Excavation begins
French archaeology, and then a UNESCO inscription in 1997.

One mosaic at Volubilis shows a desultor: a rider jumping between two moving horses at full gallop. Somebody liked the trick enough to put it on a dining-room floor, in a province whose horses Rome imported.
