The cone is a condenser

A tagine recycles its own water. Steam leaves the food, hits the cool upper clay, turns back into liquid and runs down the slope onto the food again. It was worked out in a place where both water and firewood had to be carried.

Updated 7 September 2026 Technical description of the vessel. Recipes are elsewhere.

In section

The cycle, in 5 steps

01
Low heat under a wide, shallow base
Ten to fourteen inches across, so everything lies in a single layer and touches the hot floor of the dish.
02
The food releases its own moisture
Onions, tomatoes and squash give up enough that many vegetable tagines need no added liquid beyond a splash.
03
Steam climbs the cone
Which is tall and narrow, so the vapour travels a long way from the heat before it reaches the top.
04
It condenses on the cool clay
The upper cone is the coldest surface in the system. Vapour meeting it turns back to water.
05
And runs back down the inside
Down the slope, onto the food, continuously. The pot bastes itself for as long as the fire is lit.

The small knob at the summit is a vent that keeps pressure from building. It is not a reservoir. Some lids do have a hollow handle meant to be filled with cold water, which cools the apex and makes the condensation more aggressive, but that water never touches the food.

What it was solving for

Water
Scarce, and carried. So the pot is built to lose almost none of it
Fuel
Scarce. A small charcoal fire runs it for hours
Cheap cuts
Shoulder, shank, neck. High in collagen, inedible fast and excellent slow
Portable
An oven that can be carried, set down anywhere and cooked on

Long, low, wet heat turns collagen into gelatin, which is the whole mechanism behind the texture. The tagine holds a temperature just under a boil for hours without attention, and that is exactly the condition collagen needs.

Clay does not like surprises

RuleWhy
Never cold liquid into a hot pot, or hot stock into a cold oneThermal shock cracks fired clay. Temperature has to move gradually in both directions
Start it in a cold oven and bring both up togetherSame reason
Use a diffuser on gasA flame licking one spot on a clay base is a crack waiting
Set it down on wood, never stoneA cold marble counter under a hot tagine does the damage in seconds
Season a new one before first useSoak overnight, then simmer with oil and water to close the pores

Where it comes from

The vessel is Amazigh and most often placed in the Anti-Atlas, where the constraints it answers are sharpest. Nineteenth-century European travellers recorded them in use across Algeria and Tunisia as well: red clay, finished with pine resin and olive oil.

The base is also the serving dish. It comes off the fire and onto the table, and everyone eats from the same one with bread, which means the pot is doing three jobs at once and was never washed up in between.
