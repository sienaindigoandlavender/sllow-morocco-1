Founded by a woman in 859, restored by a woman in 2016

Fatima al-Fihri spent her inheritance on a mosque and a school in Fes. The library attached to it has never closed since. Eleven centuries later the Ministry of Culture handed the restoration to Aziza Chaouni, who found a river running under the floor.

Updated 7 September 2026 Reopening is dated to 2016 in most accounts and to 2017 in others.

The two of them

Fatima al-Fihri
Founded it, 859
Her family came to Fes from Qayrawan in what is now Tunisia — the name al-Qarawiyyin records where they were from. She and her sister were educated. When their father died she put the whole of her inheritance into a mosque and a centre of learning for the city she had moved to.
Aziza Chaouni
Restored it, 2012–2016
A Moroccan-Canadian architect and engineer, from Fes, commissioned by the Ministry of Culture. She had not heard of the library before she was asked to save it. She insisted the restoration include opening it to the public.

The line it sits on

Ibn Khaldun studied here in the fourteenth century, and the Muqaddimah is in the collection. So is a ninth-century Quran, and a manuscript on jurisprudence by Averroes. Around four thousand rare books and manuscripts in total, on theology, law, grammar, medicine and astronomy.

The red band is the modern gap. After independence the university joined the state system and moved out of the medina in 1963, taking its teaching and its readers with it. The new campus had its own library. The old one kept its manuscripts and lost its traffic.

What was wrong with it

The river is the detail that stops people. Excavating the floor turned up water running underneath the building, which together with a blocked drainage system and centuries of rain coming off the neighbouring mosque roof had been working on the structure the whole time. She built an underground canal to take it to the sewer.

She also found rooms nobody had recorded, including a chamber by which important visitors had entered.

The claim, stated precisely

Al-Qarawiyyin is generally described as the oldest continuously operating library in the world, and the university as the oldest continuously operating degree-granting institution. Both claims turn on the word continuously: there are older foundations, and none of them ran without interruption.

Which is why the 1963 move matters and why the restoration does. The thread was thinning. It did not break.
