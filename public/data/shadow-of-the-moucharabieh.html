1 screen, 4 jobs

A moucharabieh is not a decorative window. It cools the room, ventilates it, breaks the light into strips, and lets whoever is inside watch the lane without being watched back. Four problems, solved by one piece of turned cedar.

Updated 7 September 2026 Technical description of the type. Regional variants differ in spindle size and spacing.

In section

The screen sits in the thickness of the wall, so it works as a depth rather than a surface. Air passing through it slows and spreads. Light passing through it arrives as strips rather than a glare. And the eye passing through it goes one way only.

The four

Light
The lattice cuts direct sun into small beams and drops the contrast, so a room lit through it is bright without being hot or glaring.
Air
Small apertures speed the air passing through and increase the surface it touches, which cools it. Porous water jars set on the sill cool it further by evaporation.
Sight
From the dark side you see the bright side clearly. From the bright side the lattice reads as solid. The asymmetry is optical, not moral.
Modesty
Which is what that asymmetry was used for: a household could look out onto the street, and be part of it, without being on display in it.

How it is made

ElementDetail
The spindlesTurned on a bow lathe, one at a time, in cedar. Each is a small column with a repeated profile
The jointsDry. The pieces are cut to fit and assembled without glue or nails, so the whole panel can move with humidity instead of splitting
The woodAtlas cedar, from the forests described elsewhere in this archive. Aromatic, stable, and resistant to insects
The densityTighter near eye level, more open above. The screen is graded to what each height is for

The dry joint is the clever part. A rigid glued panel in a courtyard that swings thirty degrees between night and afternoon would come apart within a few seasons. A loose assembly of hundreds of small pieces absorbs the movement across all of them.

The same idea, other names

WhereWhat it is called
Morocco and the MaghrebMoucharabieh, from the Arabic mashrabiya
Egypt and the LevantMashrabiya. One derivation ties the word to the place a drinking jar was kept, cooling in the draught
Al-Andalus and later SpainCelosía, and the same logic in plaster and brick
IndiaJali, cut from stone rather than turned from wood

The jar derivation is worth keeping even if it is only one of several offered, because it describes what the ledge actually did: porous earthenware set in a moving draught, cooling water and the room at once.

Where to look

Upper floors, over lanes, on the street side of a house rather than the courtyard side. In Fes and Tetouan the panels are older and the spindles finer. In Marrakech they are frequently newer work in the same idiom, and the medina has cabinetmakers still turning them.

A riad has almost none, because it does not need any. The whole house faces inward onto its own courtyard, so there is nothing to screen. The moucharabieh belongs to the elevations that meet the public lane, which is why it reads as the face a Moroccan house shows the street.
