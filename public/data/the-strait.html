The narrowest crossing is also the deepest

Fourteen kilometres of water separate two continents, and directly beneath that gap the seabed drops to nine hundred metres. That single fact is why a tunnel under the Strait of Gibraltar has been studied since 1930 and not yet built, and why the current plan crosses at twenty-eight kilometres instead.

Updated 7 September 2026 Cost and depth figures vary between published studies; ranges are given.

The problem, in section

The shortest route is unusable, because the trough sits exactly where the shores come closest. So the alignment moves west, from Punta Paloma to Malabata, and crosses a broader but shallower stretch: twenty-eight kilometres of water, with the tunnel reaching about four hundred and seventy-five metres below the surface at its lowest.

The 1930s surveys ran into the same wall from the other side. They found very hard rock under the strait, which the machinery of the day could not cut. An immersed tube was proposed instead, and the depth ruled it out. A century of engineering has been spent going around one trough.

Two running tunnels and a third for service and evacuation, on the Channel Tunnel pattern. It would join the Spanish network directly to Al Boraq, which is the reason the project came back: the 2018 line proved Morocco could build and run high-speed rail, and gave the tunnel something to connect to.

The comparison that matters is the first one, not the last. A tunnel does not beat a plane on time and is not meant to. It beats a plane on freight, on frequency, and on the fact that a train from Casablanca could arrive in Madrid without anything being unloaded at a port.

Ninety-six years of studies

1930
First proposed
Spanish surveys map the geology and find rock too hard for the machinery of the period.
1979
The two kings sign
Juan Carlos I and Hassan II agree to develop the link. Feasibility work runs to 1981.
2009
Shelved
The Europe–Africa fixed link proposals go quiet.
2018
Al Boraq opens
Africa’s first high-speed line, and the thing that makes the tunnel worth building.
2023
The joint commission meets again
For the first time in fourteen years. Spain commits €2.3 m to design work.
2025
Herrenknecht reports
The world’s leading maker of tunnel boring machines concludes that current technology can do it. SNED and SECEGSA work in parallel on either side.
2027
The decision
Both governments are reported to be aiming for a decision on tendering a first exploratory tunnel.
