A Roman safety pin, kept for 1,700 years

The fibula arrived with the legions as a fastener for a cloak. Rome left in 285 and the pin stayed, the way the calendar stayed. Amazigh silversmiths turned it into the central object of a woman’s wardrobe, and Jewish silversmiths from Spain taught them to enamel it.

Updated 7 September 2026 Symbol readings vary between tribes and are reported, not fixed.

The object

A pair, one at each shoulder, joined by a chain that takes the weight across the chest. The plate is triangular. The pin is long and it works: this fastens a garment, and everything else on it is added to a thing that has a job.

4 inheritances, in 1 object

Roman
The fibula itself. A Latin word and a Roman garment fastener, essentially an early safety pin, left behind by the province described elsewhere in this archive.
Carthaginian
The khamsa. The open hand was already an averter of evil for Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Jews and Romans, long before Islam gave it the name Hand of Fatima.
Andalusi
The enamel. Champlevé work in blue, green and red is held to have come from Iberia with Jewish silversmiths, and became a hallmark of North African silver.
Amazigh
Everything else. The triangle, the tribal grammar of motifs, and the decision that a fastener should be the most important thing a woman owns.

The hand, read twice

Khamsa means five. The same object is read two ways in the same country, and has been for a very long time.

Read asThe five are
Hand of FatimaThe five pillars: faith, prayer, alms, fasting, pilgrimage. Named for the Prophet’s daughter
Hand of MiriamThe five books of the Torah. Named for the sister of Moses
Older than bothA protective hand in Phoenician, Carthaginian and Roman use, adopted rather than invented by either faith

What the plate says

Readings differ between confederations and no fixed dictionary exists. These are the interpretations most often reported, and they should be taken as that.

The triangle
Woman, and the tent. Fertility and the household in one shape
The rosette
The sun, at the centre of the plate
The crescent
The moon. Protection, and read as feminine
Enclosed circles
To hold negative energy inside and keep it from reaching the wearer
The hirz
A box amulet, often the centrepiece of a Jewish woman’s fibula in the Anti-Atlas. Enamelled green and yellow, and containing a written text

Why Tiznit

Tiznit is the silver capital of Morocco because Jewish families settled there in the early 1500s and the trade passed down inside those families for four hundred years. Fes, Essaouira and Tiznit all had Jewish silversmiths working alongside Amazigh ones, and the exchange ran both ways.

Silver came up from sub-Saharan Africa and across the Mediterranean. Amber, coral and glass beads came from further still. A fibula from the Rif and one from the Anti-Atlas differ enough that a woman’s tribe could be read off her chest at a distance, which is the function nobody lists: it was an identity document you could see across a market.

The earrings that are not earrings

Many Amazigh temporals are too heavy to hang from an earlobe. They were hung either side of the head from a band crossing the crown, sitting at the temple, which is what the name records. Enamelled, set with glass cabochons, and carrying their weight on the skull rather than the ear.
