4.34 kilos a year, of a plant that does not grow here

Morocco is among the largest importers of green tea in the world and grows none of it. How it got that way is usually explained by a blockade and a stranded cargo, and the customs records tell a slower and better story.

Updated 7 September 2026 Historical. The import figures are from customs records at Mogador.

2 accounts of how it arrived

What is repeated

In 1854 the Baltic ports close. British merchants are left holding cargoes of Chinese gunpowder green with nowhere to sell it.

They offload the lot at Tangier and Mogador. Moroccans take to it at once, add mint and sugar, and a national drink is born inside a decade.

Graham Cornwell, whose Sweetening the Pot is the one proper history of tea and sugar in Morocco, went looking for the origin of this claim. His finding: none of the authors who advance it offer any evidence or cite any source.

What the record shows

Tea arrives around the turn of the eighteenth century, when Queen Anne sends chests of it to Sultan Moulay Ismail, in the course of negotiating the release of English captives.

He keeps it. A tea division is established at the palace. For most of a century it stays a court and elite drink, and some religious figures object to it.

Then the price falls, and it falls a long way. That is the event, and it takes fifty years rather than one shipment.

What actually happened, in two numbers

Tea landing at the port of Mogador, between 1857 and the end of the century. The volume multiplied twenty-five times. The value of it multiplied eight.

The gap between those two bars is the price collapsing. Tea stopped being a luxury because it got cheap, and it got cheap because global supply expanded and shipping improved. Nobody had to dump a cargo.

Where the war does come in

Sideways, and through wheat. Britain needed cereals to feed an army in the Crimea. Moroccan grain exports reached their highest level in decades in 1856, and the trade balance between the two countries moved with them. More British trade with Morocco meant more British goods arriving, tea among them.

Which is a duller mechanism and a better one, because there are customs records behind it.

Why green, and why Chinese

Britain spent the nineteenth century building tea plantations in India and went on selling Morocco Chinese leaf regardless.
