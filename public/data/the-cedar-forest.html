Morocco holds the largest Atlas cedar forest on earth

Cedrus atlantica grows in two countries and nowhere else, and the great majority of it is here. Inside that forest lives the only macaque species outside Asia, along with servals, Cuvier’s gazelle and the last Maghreb leopards anybody has evidence for.

Updated 7 September 2026 Population figures are from published survey work; the most recent full census is 2007–2009.

Where the forest sits

The cedar takes a band roughly a thousand metres deep, between 1,170 and 2,200 metres, where there is enough winter rain and snow and not yet too much cold. Below it is holm oak and thuya, above it juniper. Nothing else in Morocco occupies that band.

The top of its range

Morocco has the largest surviving area of the species anywhere. The Rif once held one of the great cedar forests and now accounts for about 15% of the national total; the centre of gravity has moved to the Middle Atlas and the eastern High Atlas.

The macaque

Macaca sylvanus is the only macaque anywhere outside Asia and the only wild primate in Morocco. Between 65 and 75% of the surviving population lives in these cedar forests, which makes the tree and the animal a single conservation question.

The first two figures are global, the last two Moroccan, so the bars are not strictly comparable. What the survey work agrees on is the direction. Line transects over 244 kilometres in 2005 found densities of 12 to 28 animals per square kilometre where earlier work had found 43 to 70.

The species is on CITES Appendix II, and Ifrane National Park protects the densest remaining stand.

Where it is being replanted

Large reforestation campaigns have been running in Ifrane province, which is also where Ifrane National Park protects the densest remaining stand. Grazing restrictions, fencing, zonation and forest guarding have all been trialled here, and the replanting is the part that has scaled.
