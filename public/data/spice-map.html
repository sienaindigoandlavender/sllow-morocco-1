150,000 flowers

That is what one kilogram of saffron costs in labour. Each crocus gives three threads, they are pulled by hand at dawn in late October, and the arithmetic is the reason the spice is faked more often than any other thing sold in a Moroccan souk.

Taliouine, in the Anti-Atlas, is where almost all of it is grown. The separating of thread from flower is done by women, sitting, by hand, for the six weeks the harvest lasts.

What things actually cost

Two prices for the same product: what a quality shop charges, and what a tourist-facing stall opens at. Both are real; the difference is who is standing in front of the counter.

Saffron is the one thing to leave un-bargained. A price low enough to feel like a win indicates safflower, dyed corn silk, or low-grade threads cut with stem.

Telling real from not

Real saffron
  • Deep crimson, with a paler tip where the thread meets the style
  • Slightly sticky rather than brittle
  • A warm, faintly honeyed smell, complex rather than sharp
  • Sold in very small quantities at a serious price
  • Colours water slowly, over minutes, not instantly
Not saffron
  • Uniform bright red from root to tip
  • Smells of nothing, or of dye
  • Offered by the handful
  • Turns water red immediately
  • Safflower, called carthamus, is the usual substitute

Ras el hanout, and the ingredient-count fallacy

The name means head of the shop: the best a merchant has. There is no recipe, every seller mixes his own, and no two are alike. Twelve to thirty spices is the working range.

Stalls advertising fifty or a hundred spices are quoting a number for its own sake, since past roughly thirty ingredients nothing further is detectable on the palate, and a blend that smells overwhelmingly of cinnamon has been made carelessly whatever the count.

Blends shift by city. Marrakech tends hotter, Fes more floral. Buy whole and unground where you can: ground spice loses its volatile oils within weeks, and a jar six months old is mostly colour.
