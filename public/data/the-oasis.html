3 harvests under 1 canopy

A Moroccan oasis is a piece of engineering: date palms cut the sun and hold the humidity, fruit trees live in the shade the palms make, and vegetables and forage grow in the damp underneath both. Take the top storey away and the two below it die.

Updated 7 September 2026 Loss estimates range from one third to two thirds over a century; both are given.

How it is built

The palm is not there for the dates alone. It is a climate device. Under a closed palm canopy the air is measurably cooler and wetter than a few metres outside it, and that difference is what allows pomegranate, fig, olive and almond to fruit here, and cereals and forage to grow below them. Three crops on one footprint, on the same water.

Most of them lie in the desert basin south of the Atlas, strung along the old caravan routes described elsewhere in this archive. The oases are where the routes could stop, which is why they are where they are.

The loop nobody intends to start

This is the mechanism that turns a dry year into a lost field, and it is why restoring the khettara matters so much. Deeper water is more mineralised, so irrigating with it raises the salt in the soil. A gravity gallery draws from a shallower, sweeter part of the aquifer and breaks the cycle.

A study of the Ternata palm grove in the middle Draa recorded palm cover falling by around seventy per cent over the period it covered, which is the scale the restoration programmes are working against.

What is being done

The response has largely been to repair the old system rather than replace it, which is the right instinct: the khettara worked for eight hundred years without power.

Sadok is the useful case. A hotel owner who turned farmer for lack of guests, working sterile ground for fifteen years on the argument that a palm is a wall. Seven hectares is not a national policy. It is a demonstration that the ground comes back if somebody stays on it.
