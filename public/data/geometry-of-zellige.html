The 50-point star has 48 points

Zellige stars are named for how many points they have, and the names are approximate. A khamsini, a fifty, carries forty-eight. A mi’ini, a hundred, carries ninety-six. The geometry requires the count to divide by six, so the round numbers were never available.

Updated 7 September 2026 Historical and technical. Workshop practice varies between Fes and Meknes.

Every piece is chipped off by hand

furmah
The individual cut pieces. Every one is chipped from a larger fired tile by hand, with a pointed hammer called a menkach.
naqsh
The pattern, drawn on paper first. The maalem works to it from memory as much as from the sheet.
face down
The panel is assembled upside down on a flat surface, glaze against the bench, and plaster is poured over the back. Nobody sees the finished face until it is turned over.

The rosette above is seventeen pieces. A wall in the Bou Inania is hundreds of thousands, and the apprenticeship to cut them takes eight to ten years.

Separate sequences run on five-fold and eight-fold symmetry. And beyond the stars there is a whole vocabulary that has nothing to do with them: honeycombs, webs, checkerboards, and the pattern the craft calls steps and shoulders.

The colours arrived in order

Zellige began in two colours and took seven centuries to reach the palette people now think of as Moroccan. Each one waited on a glaze chemistry.

3 traditions people confuse

Islamic geometric tilework is not one system. These three use different shapes and different construction logic, and they belong to different places.

TraditionWhat it is
ZelligeMaghrebi. Cut glazed terracotta, assembled face down into plaster. Fes and Meknes from the 10th or 11th century
GirihPersian. Built from five standard tile shapes with strapwork drawn over them. The system behind the ten-fold stars of Isfahan
SebkaAndalusian. A lozenge grid used as a surface treatment on walls and minarets, including the Koutoubia and the Giralda

The workshops of the Ain Khail quarter in Fes have been cutting zellige since the medieval period, and several of the families working there now trace the craft through their own line. When the Moors were expelled from Spain, cutters came back across with Andalusian refinements, which is why the Alhambra and the Fes madrasas read as cousins.

Why geometry and not pictures

Figurative images are avoided in Islamic religious art, which left geometry, calligraphy and plant form as the available languages. The Maghreb had inherited the mathematics to go a long way with the first of them.

The tessellations at the Alhambra, the same family of patterns, are what M. C. Escher went to Granada to study in the 1920s and 1930s. He came home and spent the rest of his career on them.
