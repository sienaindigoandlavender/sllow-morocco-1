2 Goncourts, and a man who could not read until he was 20

Morocco has produced two winners of France’s highest literary prize and one of the great autobiographies in modern Arabic, written by a man who learned the alphabet at twenty in a Tangier jail. It has also been written about, at length, by people who came and stayed for decades.

Two bands, one century

The upper band is Moroccan. The lower band is people who arrived. Notice the overlap in the 1950s: the years when Choukri was living the life he would later write, and Burroughs was a mile away in Tangier writing something else entirely.

The Moroccans

WriterWhat they did
Driss ChraïbiLe Passé simple, 1954. A first novel that took apart the patriarchal household from inside it, published in French two years before independence, and controversial in Morocco for decades
Mohamed ChoukriAl-Khubz al-Hafi, For Bread Alone. Written in Arabic, translated into English by Paul Bowles in 1973, and published in Arabic much later. An account of childhood destitution in the Rif and Tangier
Fatema MernissiSociologist, and the author of Dreams of Trespass. Wrote about the harem she grew up in as a spatial and legal arrangement rather than a fantasy
Tahar Ben JellounPrix Goncourt 1987 for La Nuit sacrée. The most widely translated Moroccan writer alive
Abdelfattah KilitoEssayist and scholar of classical Arabic literature, and the writer other writers read
Leïla SlimaniPrix Goncourt 2016 for Chanson douce, and since then a trilogy set across Moroccan independence

Most of them wrote in French, which is the fact underneath the whole list. A generation educated in a colonial school system produced a literature in the coloniser’s language that was read in Paris before it was read at home, and the following generation has been steadily moving that back into Arabic and Darija.

For Bread Alone reached the world in English first because the Arabic edition could not be published. It is the book most often named when Moroccans are asked for the one that tells the truth, and its author came to literature from complete illiteracy in his twenties.

The Bowles case is the interesting one, because he did both things. He wrote his own Morocco in The Sheltering Sky, and he also spent decades recording and translating Moroccan voices that would not otherwise have reached print in any language.

What the timeline shows

Tangier in the 1950s had an unusual concentration: an international zone with light governance, cheap living and a permissive reputation, which collected foreign writers in numbers no other North African city managed.

What is easy to miss is that the Moroccan band starts at almost the same moment. Chraïbi published in 1954, the year Burroughs arrived. The two literatures were being made in the same country at the same time, and for a long while only one of them was being read abroad.
