5 explanations, and only 1 of them is testable

Every guide in Chefchaouen has an answer and they do not agree. Rather than pick one, here is each claim set against what the documentary record actually contains — which turns out to be less than anyone assumes, and later.

Updated 7 September 2026 The strength scores are this archive’s reading of the evidence, not a published consensus.

The claims, weighed

The one that can be checked

The mosquito explanation is false. Mosquitoes locate a host by body heat, exhaled carbon dioxide and skin odour. Wall colour is not among the cues, and there is no experimental evidence that blue paint repels them. The claim survives because residents of blue quarters reported fewer mosquitoes, which is the kind of observation that produces a durable folk explanation and does not survive a controlled test.

The cooling claim is half true and does not do the work asked of it. Pale surfaces reflect more sunlight than dark ones, so a light wall is a cooler wall — but whitewash does that better than blue, and Chefchaouen sits at altitude in the Rif where the summers are already mild. It explains painting the houses pale. It does not explain painting them blue.

When the blue actually shows up

This is the part that settles it. Chefchaouen was founded in 1471 as a fortress against Portuguese expansion, and it was closed to outsiders for most of its life: three Christians are recorded as having entered in four hundred years, and the town did not open until 1920. When the Spanish finally arrived and wrote it up, they described a medieval town and did not describe a blue one.

The earliest photographs, from the 1930s, show blue — but patches of it, not the saturated monochrome that exists now. The colour deepens across the twentieth century and accelerates once visitors start coming.

What the strongest version looks like

Blue is tekhelet, the colour of the sky and therefore of the divine, woven into prayer shawls and used across Jewish practice. Chefchaouen had a Jewish population from 1492 and it grew again in the 1930s as European Jews arrived ahead of the Nazis. The photographic record and the reported intensification line up with the second date, not the first.

StageWhat happened
OriginThe practice takes hold in the mellah. Older residents consistently say only the Jewish quarter was blue until relatively recently
AdoptionNeighbours copy it, for reasons that are practical, aesthetic, or simply proximate. In Islamic usage blue carries its own associations of calm and good fortune
DepartureThe community leaves for Israel after 1948. The painting continues without them
AmplificationTourism arrives, the municipality encourages the colour, and the blue reaches the saturation visitors now photograph

Every spring the municipality still hands out brushes. The people repainting are Amazigh, Muslim, and the Spanish-speaking descendants of the other 1492 exodus. The tradition outlived the community that started it by about eighty years, which is the genuinely interesting fact and the one that never makes the caption.
