The strait was crossed in both directions

Moroccan armies took Iberia in 711 and Moroccan dynasties ruled both shores for two centuries. Eight hundred years later the traffic reversed, and the people who came back rebuilt Fes, Tetouan and Rabat in the image of the cities they had lost.

Compiled 7 September 2026 Historical. Morisco expulsion totals are estimates and vary by source.

Eight centuries, two directions

North, into Iberia South, into Morocco
711
Tariq ibn Ziyad crosses the strait
The rock he landed at is still called after him: Jebel Tariq, which English turned into Gibraltar.
1086–1269
Almoravids, then Almohads
Two Moroccan dynasties governing both shores. One architectural world, which is why the Koutoubia and the Giralda are sisters.
1471
Chefchaouen founded
Built as a refuge for Moorish and Jewish exiles, twenty-one years before Granada fell.
1492
Granada falls
The first great exodus. Muslims and Jews cross together and settle in the same northern cities.
1609–1614
The Moriscos are expelled
Philip III orders out the converted Muslims. An estimated 300,000 people leave Spain; a large share of them arrive in Morocco.
1610
The Bou Regreg Republic
Exiles from the Extremaduran town of Hornachos settle at Rabat and Salé and found a semi-independent corsair state. They built the Kasbah of the Udayas walls that visitors climb today.

Where they landed

The upper dashed line is the crossing from Granada. The lower one is Hornachos to Rabat. The northern Moroccan cities were not merely settled by these people; several were rebuilt by them.

What crossed with them

The refugees were artisans, musicians, scholars, architects and merchants rather than a general population, which is why the transfer was so concentrated.

The languages kept the marks

Andalusi Arabic died as a spoken language and left fingerprints on both sides of the water.

Two communities, one crossing

Muslims and Jews were expelled by the same decrees and arrived in the same cities. The Jewish Megorashim went on to play a central role in performing and preserving Andalusian music in Fes, Rabat and Tetouan, and the repertoire stayed shared long after the communities separated.

The 2011 constitution names the Andalusian and Hebraic components of Moroccan identity explicitly, alongside the Arab-Islamic, Amazigh and Saharan-Hassani ones.
