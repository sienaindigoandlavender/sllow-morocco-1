4 towers, 1 door, and it turns

A kasbah is a fortified family house with a tower at each corner, a single entrance that bends so nobody can see or shoot straight through it, and windows set high and small. Every decision in it is defensive, and it is also somebody’s home.

Updated 7 September 2026 The plan and elevation are typical of the Draa and Dades, not of any single building.

Plan and elevation

Rooms wrapped around a courtyard, four corner towers, and a bent entrance marked in red on the plan. Outside, walls that lean inward as they rise, crenellated tops, small high windows, and bands of decorative brickwork concentrated on the towers where the wall is thickest.

The inward lean is structural before it is aesthetic. A rammed-earth wall is strongest when it is widest at the base, and the taper is what lets the towers go four or five storeys.

Kasbah, ksar, agadir

Kasbah
One fortified household. A family, its animals and its stores, behind its own wall
Ksar
A whole village inside one wall, containing many houses. Plural ksour
Agadir
A collective fortified granary, often on a crag. Storage rather than living
Tighremt
The Tamazight word for what Arabic calls a kasbah, and the one used locally

The agadir is the one visitors rarely see and the one that explains the others. Each family held a locked cell inside it for grain, oil and documents, with a council keeping the keys and a written charter governing access. A bank, built of earth, on a rock.

The route

Two valleys, not one. The Dades runs east from Ouarzazate through Skoura, Kelaa M’Gouna and Boumalne to Tinghir. The Draa runs south through Agdz to Zagora and on toward the desert. The name covers both.

They are strung along water, which is the whole explanation for where the kasbahs are. Every one of them sits where a khettara or a river made irrigation possible, and the palmeraie came first.

Why so many, and why there

ReasonDetail
The caravan roadThese valleys are the last inhabited ground before the Sahara, on the routes described elsewhere in this archive. Goods passing through needed to be housed and guarded
Distance from any authorityFar from the imperial cities, security was a household matter. If you wanted a wall, you built one
The materialEarth, straw and water, from the field beside the site. A family could build without buying anything
Standing waterWhich meant grain, dates and something worth defending

Taourirt at Ouarzazate is the one everybody photographs, and it is not typical: it belonged to the Glaoui and is closer to a palace than a farmhouse. The ordinary kasbah is a working building on a field edge, and there are hundreds of those for every one like Taourirt.
