7 men, 7 days, 1 circle

Marrakech holds the graves of more than two hundred saints. Seven of them were chosen in the seventeenth century and given a day of the week each, on a route that circles the medina from the south-east round to the south-west. The city took its other name from them: Sab’atu Rijal, the seven men.

Updated 7 September 2026 Shrine positions are schematic. Interior access is restricted at several of them.

The circuit

Tuesday to the following Monday, one tomb a day, always in the same order. The route leaves the walls, comes back in, goes out again at Bab Aylan and returns by Bab Taghzout. That it is a circle is the point: the form deliberately echoes the circling of the Kaaba.

Sidi Bel Abbès

He is the one with a life people can tell you. A disciple of Qadi Ayyad, a friend of Averroes, and a man who lived forty years in a cave on the hill at Gueliz without once going into the city he now belongs to. He spent that life on the poor and specifically on the blind, and the shrine still carries that association.

The devotion is practical rather than ceremonial. Sellers of sfenj give him the first of the day’s frying. Farmers give him the first sheaf of the wheat. Nobody organises this; it simply continues.

An older pilgrimage already existed on the Atlantic near Essaouira: the Regraga, a forty-day spring circuit of Berber shrines, with a tent of fan palm fibre dyed in henna and a muqaddim riding a white horse at the head of it. Moulay Ismail wanted Marrakech to have its own, and asked the scholar Abu Ali al-Hasan al-Yusi to construct one.

Seven was chosen to echo the Regraga’s seven founders. A week was chosen because a working person can do a week. And the name stuck to the city itself, which is the part that outlasted the politics: ask where Sab’atu Rijal is and you are asking for Marrakech.

What it is now

Fewer people walk the full week than once did, and the shrines and their zawiyas remain among the most active places in the medina. Successive sultans, including several with quite different religious leanings, restored and enlarged the tombs rather than leaving them.

Most visitors now do the circuit in a day rather than a week, on foot, which works because the seven are all inside or against the walls. Several interiors are not open to non-Muslims; the route, the quarters and the gates are open to everyone.
