1 metre of fall per kilometre, dug blind

A khettara is a tunnel that walks water downhill out of an aquifer, using no pump and no power. Men dug them by hand, underground, holding a gradient of about one in a thousand over distances up to fifteen kilometres.

Updated 7 September 2026 Historical, with current survival figures from field inventories.

How one works

The mother well is sunk into the water table first, ten to fifty metres down. The gallery is then cut back towards the fields, and vertical shafts are driven at intervals to remove spoil and let air in. The spoil piles up in rings around each shaft mouth, which is why a khettara reads from the air as a dotted line across bare ground.

The same idea, 6 names

The technique began as the qanat in Persia around three thousand years ago and travelled with Islam across a quarter of the world. Morocco is its western end.

Some form of it is still in use in more than thirty arid countries.

The Tafilalet network went in after Sijilmasa broke up. A city that had lived on caravan tolls turned into an oasis that lived on its own water.

2 provinces, 2 outcomes

Khettaras ran the southern oases until the early 1970s, when motor pumps and new policy arrived. What happened next depended on how hard the aquifer beneath was pumped.

Roughly half the inventoried khettaras still carry water. Collective management largely held.
Haouz, around Marrakech
almost none
Hundreds of tunnels once watered the palmeraie. The water table fell, and the galleries are now dry trenches, collapsed shafts and dumping ground.

One detail is worth holding onto. When the rains came back to the south-eastern Atlas between 2009 and 2013, the water table rose and the khettaras that had survived started flowing again. The system is dormant where it has not been destroyed.
