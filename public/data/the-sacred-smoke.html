2 of these grew in Morocco

Open a bakhour tin in Marrakech and most of what is inside came by sea: benzoin from Sumatra, frankincense from the Gulf of Aden, sandalwood from Malabar. Only the harmal seed and the fasoukh resin are Moroccan. The smell of a Moroccan house is an Indian Ocean trade route.

Updated 7 September 2026 Blends vary by household and by attar. Names given in the Moroccan usage.

Where it comes from

Frankincense and myrrh out of the Horn and southern Arabia, sandalwood from the Malabar coast, benzoin from Sumatra and the Mekong. These are the oldest luxury goods there are, and Morocco is the far western end of the line that carried them.

The word jawi is the proof. Moroccans call benzoin after Java, because that is where it was understood to come from, and the name survived the journey better than the geography did.

How it is actually used

Heated
Not lit. The resin sits above a coal on foil or a screen and smoulders
Passed
The mabkhara goes round the room so guests can take the smoke into clothes and hair
Friday
And at births, weddings, and after the house is cleaned
Harmal
Thrown on alone, crackling, against the eye

The distinction between heating and burning matters. A flame destroys the volatile compounds; a coal at a distance releases them slowly, which is why the smoke is thick and low and why a piece the size of a fingernail scents a whole room.

Being offered the burner is not a courtesy at the edge of a visit. It is the visit acknowledging you, and the correct response is to lean into it.

The two Moroccan ones

MaterialWhat it is
Harmal, Peganum harmalaSyrian rue. The seeds are thrown straight onto the coal, where they pop. Used against the evil eye, at thresholds, and around a newborn. The sound is as much the point as the smell
FasoukhWhite fasoukh is the resin of Ferula communis, the giant fennel that grows across the Maghreb. Black fasoukh is a compounded blend whose recipe changes from tradition to tradition and town to town

Both belong to household practice rather than to perfumery, and both are sold by the attarine described elsewhere in this archive, alongside the plants of the pharmacopoeia. The same shop supplies the remedy and the thing burned over it.

Amber is not amber

The grey or honey-coloured lumps sold as ambre in the souk are not fossil resin and never were. They are a made thing: benzoin, labdanum and gum worked together and pressed, sold by weight and burned like any other bakhour.

That is not a substitution or a lesser version. Amber in perfumery has always meant an accord rather than a material, and the Moroccan lump is one of the oldest ways of making it.
