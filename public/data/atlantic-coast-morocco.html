The wind blows the sea away, and the fish arrive

Morocco’s Atlantic is cold for its latitude, and that is the reason it is one of the richest fishing grounds on earth. The trade wind pushes warm surface water offshore, deep water rises to replace it, and everything that lives there is living on what comes up.

Updated 7 September 2026 Physical description of the Canary Current upwelling system.

How it works

The wind runs along the coast rather than at it. Because of the earth’s rotation the water it drags is deflected offshore, and the gap has to be filled from below. What rises is cold, and it is loaded with the nitrate and phosphate that has been sinking out of the sunlit layer for years.

Nutrient plus daylight is all a plankton bloom needs. The bloom feeds the sardine, the sardine feeds everything else, and the whole coast from Tangier to Dakhla runs on that one exchange.

There are only four coasts on earth that do this at scale: off Peru, off California, off Namibia, and off north-west Africa. All four are on the eastern side of an ocean, all four are cold for their latitude, and all four are famous for fish out of proportion to their size.

That last line is the connection most people miss. The argan forest described elsewhere in this archive is not simply surviving in a dry place. It is positioned where cold water makes fog, and the fog is part of its water supply.

Two oceans, one corner

Morocco is one of very few countries with a Mediterranean coast and an Atlantic one, and the two behave nothing alike. The Mediterranean side is warm, tideless in effect, and clear. The Atlantic side is cold, has a genuine tidal range, and is opaque with the life in it.

The strait between them is fourteen kilometres wide, and the two bodies of water it joins are as different as any two seas that touch anywhere.
