2 homes, 1 mountain

Transhumance is not nomadism. The families of the High Atlas have a fixed village and a fixed summer pasture, and they move between them twice a year on the same paths their grandparents used. Up when the snow clears, down before it returns.

The section

Four bands up one slope. The plain below eight hundred metres, the terraced foothills, the cedar and holm oak between sixteen hundred and twenty-two hundred, and above that the azaghar: high open grass that is only usable for a few months and is worth the whole journey.

Fixed places, not a wandering. That is the whole distinction

A mountain has more grass than any one altitude can offer at once. Move the flock upward through the season and you follow the growth: the low ground greens first and burns off, the high ground is still under snow, and by the time the low pasture is finished the high one is ready.

Which means the flock is eating new grass for six months instead of exhausting one pasture in two. The journey is not a hardship attached to the system. It is the system.

This is the part that separates it from nomadism cleanly. Nothing is given up at either end. A transhumant family has two addresses, and the calendar decides which one is in use.

The agdal

The high pasture is not open ground. It is held under agdal: a customary rule that closes a pasture for a set period so the grass can grow, and opens it on an agreed date for the households with a right to it.

The closure is announced and enforced by the community itself, and breaking it carries a penalty. It is one of the oldest working conservation instruments anywhere, and it is the same word as the royal gardens in Marrakech, which were also an enclosure.

ElementHow it works
The closed seasonNobody grazes. The date is set locally and applies to everyone
The openingAnnounced, and often marked. The flocks go up together rather than in a race
The rightAttached to households of the valley, inherited, and not transferable to outsiders
The penaltySet and collected by the assembly, not by the state

Reading a mountain in summer

A village that looks half empty in July is not declining. It is doing what it does in July. The stone houses with shuttered doors have people two thousand metres above them, in tents, with the animals, and the same houses will be full again in November.

The tents themselves are worth looking at: goat hair and camel wool, woven by the women of the household, black and low against the wind. They are the second building of a two-building life.
