The kiln burns olive pits

Moroccan pottery is fired on the waste of Moroccan agriculture: crushed olive stones, palm frond, argan shell. The fuel is free, it burns hot and fast, and the temperature it reaches is what decides which colours the country’s potters can use at all.

Updated 7 September 2026 Description of traditional practice. Many workshops now fire on gas as well.

How the kiln works

An updraught kiln, which is the simplest arrangement that works. Fire at the bottom, a perforated floor above it, the ware stacked in the chamber, and a chimney to pull the heat up through everything on its way out. The pots nearest the floor get the most heat, so the stacking is a decision rather than a convenience.

Olive pits are the classic fuel, and they are the residue of the pressing described elsewhere in this archive. A country that produces that much olive oil produces a great deal of stone, and it burns hot enough to vitrify a glaze.

Temperature decides the palette

Cobalt
Survives almost any firing. Which is why blue is everywhere
Copper
Gives green, and shifts with the atmosphere in the kiln
Manganese
Brown to purple-black, and stable
No red
Most reds burn out. This is why traditional glazed ware is blue, green and brown

Every fired colour in the country is a mineral that can survive being cooked. That constraint, and not taste, is why the glaze palette looks the way it does across a thousand years.

Six centres, six characters

Fes
cobalt blue on white
The oldest urban tradition. Blue and white, fine drawing, and the zellige workshops next door use the same clay
Safi
polychrome, copper green
The largest producer. A whole hill of kilns above the town, and the glaze range is the widest in the country
Tamegroute
the green glaze
Manganese and copper over a low-fired body, producing a green that runs and pools. Made nowhere else
Salé
unglazed and utilitarian
Cooking vessels and storage. The work that gets used rather than displayed
The Rif
hand-built, no wheel
Women's work, coil-built, burnished and painted with a fine brush after firing
Ourika and the Atlas
red earthenware
Tagines and braziers, thrown fast and sold at the weekly souk

Tamegroute

The green of Tamegroute is made nowhere else and cannot easily be reproduced. Copper and manganese over a body fired low, in kilns beside the zaouia in the Draa, with a glaze deliberately allowed to run, pool and craze.

Every piece comes out different, which in a factory would be a fault and here is the product. The library of that same zaouia holds four thousand manuscripts, and the pottery is a few minutes from it.

The two ways of making a pot

WheelHand-built
Urban, and men’s work. Fes, Safi, SaléRural, and women’s work. The Rif and the Atlas
Fast, repeatable, and made in batches for saleCoil-built, slower, and made for the household or the local souk
Glazed and fired in a communal kilnOften burnished rather than glazed, and painted after firing
Signed by a workshopUnsigned, and the patterns run in families

The two traditions have almost nothing to do with one another. They use different tools, different firing, different decoration, and different hands. What they share is the clay.
