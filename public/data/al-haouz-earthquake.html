19 km down, 550 km from any plate boundary

The Al Haouz earthquake was not large by global standards. It was shallow, it was close, and it came at 23:11 when everyone was indoors. What followed was the largest reconstruction programme in the country’s history.

Updated 7 September 2026 Casualty and damage figures vary between sources; ranges are given.

What happened underground

An oblique-thrust rupture on a reverse fault. One side rode up over the other by as much as 1.7 metres along a thirty-kilometre section, and it did so nineteen kilometres below the surface, which is shallow. Shallow means the energy has less rock to travel through before it reaches a village.

It also happened five hundred and fifty kilometres from the boundary between the African and Eurasian plates. This is not a plate edge. It is a tight cluster of faults inside a continent, compressed and sheared at once, which is the same system that raised the High Atlas in the first place. The mountains and the earthquake have the same cause.

Why the buildings came down

Masonry and earth are strong in compression and weak in tension, which is fine under their own weight and demanding under lateral shaking. A ring beam at wall-head level and ties at the corners hold a wall to the wall it meets. Reconstruction guidance since 2023 is built around exactly that, and the diagram above is what it is designed to prevent.

Which is the finding worth carrying forward. Earth is a serious structural material, and reinforced properly it performs. The engineering answer was never to abandon it.

What has been rebuilt

A five-year national recovery programme was announced at 120 billion dirhams, about $11.7 billion, covering rehousing, infrastructure and services. Reported progress as of the most recent official update:
