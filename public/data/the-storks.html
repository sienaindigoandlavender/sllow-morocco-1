98% of first-year storks cross to Morocco

A stork cannot flap its way to Africa. It needs rising warm air to soar on, and warm air does not rise over water, so the entire western European population funnels through one 14-kilometre gap at Gibraltar and lands here.

2 gates out of Europe

Soaring birds ride thermals, columns of air heated by the ground. The sea makes none, so a direct crossing of the Mediterranean is not available to them. Everything is forced to one of two narrow places: Gibraltar in the west, the Bosporus in the east.

The dashed line follows one bird. C666 hatched at Doñana in June 2013, was tagged as a nestling, and crossed six countries before it died: Spain, Morocco, Algeria, Mauritania, Mali and Niger. It wintered in the Sahel and came back to northern Morocco.

The wind decides which end

With an easterly blowing, the birds pile up at the western end of the strait and the crossing bottlenecks. When it swings west they drift east and pass over Gibraltar itself. Watching the passage is a matter of reading the forecast rather than the calendar.

The population that stopped going

Twenty-five years of Iberian census data show the share of storks that no longer migrate at all, staying in southern Europe through the winter.

Landfill is the reason. Open refuse gives a year-round food supply and removes the need to leave. The same thing is happening on this side of the water: Moroccan storks are increasingly resident too, and the breeding population around Kenitra has grown sharply.

The adults stay, and the young still go. Among first-year Iberian birds, 98% cross the strait, which means the flyway is being maintained by juveniles learning a route their parents have stopped flying.

Nesting on the roof

The white stork is protected in Morocco and holds a status close to sacred. A nest on a house is taken as a sign of good fortune, which is why they are left alone on minarets, ruins and water towers in the middle of working towns.
